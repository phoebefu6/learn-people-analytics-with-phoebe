Surveys as evidence

Headcount is recorded, tenure is computed from a date, and pay is on a payslip. A survey score is different in kind: somebody chose to open the survey, chose to finish it, chose a number between one and five for each item, and the numbers were averaged into a score that then sits on a dashboard beside headcount with exactly the same confidence. Tonight is about what that score can carry. Who answered and who did not, which is the survey's version of the base; why a mean of 3.8 on a five-point scale can describe a team that mildly agrees and a team where one person in five chose the lowest answer; the minimum group size below which a score identifies a person; and the most quoted engagement number in the field, read with the edition it came from.

Part 0

Where we start

The running survey tonight is constructed: twelve items on a five-point scale, sent to Apex's 1,022 staff on the 1 January 2026 payroll, with 600 responses. Every response is invented, one item was deliberately built to measure almost nothing, and the pages say so wherever a survey number appears. What is real is the mechanism. Every survey has a population it was sent to and a smaller population that answered; every mean on a five-point scale hides a distribution; every cut of the results has a smallest group; and every benchmark quoted beside the score came from a particular edition of somebody else's survey. Foundations session 1 asked "who is not in this table" of an HR export. Tonight asks it of a survey, where the missing people chose to be missing.

Part 1 · covers the response funnel

Who answers, and who does not

6 min live

A survey goes to a population and comes back from a sample of it, and the sample chose itself. Apex's constructed survey went to 1,022 people and 600 answered, 58.7 percent. The score on the dashboard is an average over the 600. It is presented, almost always, as if it described the 1,022. The 422 who did not answer are not a random slice of the company, and nothing in the survey can tell you what they would have said.

LiveThe response rate is the base, and it goes under the score4 min▶

Every survey score is a flow over a stock, like every rate in Foundations session 1. The stock is the population invited, on a date: Apex's 1,022 on the 1 January 2026 payroll. The flow is the people who answered in the survey window: 600, a count chosen for the constructed survey. The response rate is 600 over 1,022, 58.7 percent, and it is the base of every number the survey produces. A score reported without it is a numerator wearing a decimal point.

The 422 are not missing at random. The people who skip a survey can be the people it most needs to hear: the ones who have stopped believing anything will change, the ones who are already interviewing elsewhere, the ones too overloaded to spend ten minutes on a form. It can also run the other way, a team so content it never bothers. Nothing in the survey can tell you which. What you can do is compare the 600 to the 1,022 on everything HR already knows about everyone: department, entity, tenure, grade. If the respondents are older in tenure than the invited, or one department answered at half the rate of the rest, the score leans towards the groups that answered, and the sentence that reports it has to say so.

A high response rate is not immunity. At 58.7 percent the score describes a majority of Apex; it still describes the majority that chose to speak. Deep-dive session 7 plants exactly this bias in the constructed survey on purpose, so that the gap between the respondents' score and everyone's score can be measured, which a real survey never allows.

Self-studyResponse rate by group, and the department that answered least3 min▶

A single response rate for the company hides the rate for each group, and the groups are where the score will be read. Compute the rate per department from the HR table, which has every invited person in it, and the survey tool, which has every respondent. The department with the lowest rate has the score most shaped by its volunteers, and it is usually the department whose score leadership is most curious about.

Two habits follow. First, print the response rate beside every group's score, not once in a footnote: "Sales: score X, answered Y percent" is a different claim from "Sales: score X". Second, treat a change in response rate as a change in the population. If a department's score rose from one year to the next while its response rate fell, the score may have risen because the unhappy stopped answering. Both years were computed correctly; the two years describe different people. The X and Y are yours to fill from your own survey; no Apex group rate is claimed.

Real world

The score that rose because people stopped answering. A team's engagement score goes up two tenths of a point in a year when three people resigned and the response rate dropped by a third. The manager is congratulated. Nobody asks who stopped answering, and the people who stopped answering were, as it later turns out, the people who were about to resign. Constructed example; the mechanism is the reason every survey score needs its response rate beside it.

Part 2 · covers what a five-point mean hides

A five-point mean is not a temperature

5 min live

A temperature of 3.8 degrees means the same thing whichever way it was reached, because degrees are equal steps on a measured scale. A survey mean of 3.8 is an average of answers from a list of five labels: strongly disagree, disagree, neither, agree, strongly agree. The labels are in order, but nobody measured whether the step from "disagree" to "neither" is the same size as the step from "agree" to "strongly agree", and the average throws away the shape. Two constructed teams answer the same item. Both average 3.8. One of them has a problem the mean cannot show.

LiveThe same mean from two distributions3 min▶

Team A: a fifth answered 3 and four fifths answered 4. Nobody is unhappy and nobody is delighted. Team B: a fifth answered 1, a twentieth each answered 2 and 3, 15 percent answered 4 and 55 percent answered 5. Both average exactly 3.8, and the two teams need opposite conversations. Team A is a mild, stable yes. Team B is a majority who are very positive and a fifth who chose the lowest answer on the scale, and a fifth of a team at the bottom of the scale is a conversation the mean will never prompt.

Three numbers carry what the mean loses: the favourable share (4 or 5), the unfavourable share (1 or 2), and the count behind them. Team A is 80 favourable and 0 unfavourable; team B is 70 and 25. A dashboard that shows those two shares for every group, with the response count beside them, will never show two identical teams that are not. Constructed teams; every survey tool can produce the same two shares from its own export.

Whether a mean of five-point answers is meaningful at all is argued, not settled. One side treats the labels as ordered categories only, where a mean is not defined in principle; the other side points out that averages of several such items behave well in practice and are used everywhere. You do not need to settle it to act on it: show the distribution beside the mean, and the argument stops mattering for the decision in front of you.

Self-studyReliability: do twelve items measure one thing?3 min▶

An engagement score is usually the average of several items, twelve in the constructed survey, on the claim that they all measure one underlying thing. That claim can be tested. Cronbach 1951, "Coefficient alpha and the internal structure of tests", Psychometrika 16(3) 297-334, gave the number that tests it: coefficient alpha, which is high when the items move together across people and low when they do not. If the items measure one thing, a person who scores high on one tends to score high on the rest.

The constructed survey has one item built on purpose to measure almost nothing: item 9. Averaged into the score, it adds noise to everyone's number by an amount unrelated to anything anybody did. The question to ask of any scale is "what is the reliability of this scale, and which item drags it down?" Deep-dive session 7 writes alpha from the 1951 formula, finds item 9 with it, and shows what dropping an item does to every score, which is a decision about the questionnaire rather than a statistical one.

Alpha says whether items hang together. It does not say they measure what their label claims, and it does not say the score causes or predicts anything. A reliable scale can be reliably measuring the wrong thing.

Real world

The two teams on one slide. A quarterly review ranks departments by mean engagement, and two departments tie at 3.8. One gets no attention; the other loses four people in the next quarter. Both results were on the slide that ranked them equal, in the one column nobody printed: the share who answered 1. Constructed example; the favourable and unfavourable shares are in every survey tool's export and take one extra column to show.

Part 3 · covers the minimum group, Gallup by edition and the two folk claims

The wall, the headline, and two sentences without a source

4 min live

Surveys promise anonymity, and a mean over three people is not anonymous: in a group of three, the people in it can often work out who said what. So every cut of the results has a minimum group size below which no score is shown. The rule sounds simple and fails in a specific way: a group that is hidden can come back by subtraction from the groups that are shown. Then two kinds of number that arrive beside a survey score from outside it, a benchmark and a folk claim, and how to tell which one has a measurement behind it.

LiveThe anonymity threshold is a minimum group size, applied to every cut3 min▶

The threshold is a count, set once and applied before any score is shown. Below it, a group's score is not displayed, because a mean over a handful of people tells the handful's manager who said what. The number itself is a policy choice with no single authoritative source; the constructed survey uses 8, the same stated choice deep-dive session 7 applies in code. Whatever the number, write it down and apply it to every group the tool can produce.

The failure is the subtraction. In the figure, HR's 40 constructed respondents split by entity into 21, 12 and 7. The 7 are below 8 and are held back. But the HR total of 40 is shown, and so are the 21 and the 12, and from those three means anybody can recover the mean of the 7: forty times the total mean, minus the two shown groups' sums, divided by seven. The fix is to hold back a second cell so that the hidden remainder is itself above the threshold: hide the 12 as well and the hidden pair is 19.

The same logic applies to cuts nobody planned. Department by tenure band by sex is a small group even when every margin is large, and a dashboard filter that lets anyone combine cuts freely will produce it on request. The threshold has to apply to what the tool can show, not to what the report designer intended.

Self-studyGallup by edition: 23, then 21, then 202 min▶

The most quoted engagement number in the field comes from Gallup's State of the Global Workplace. Gallup reported 23 percent of employees engaged in its 2024 edition, on 2023 data; its 2025 edition reported 21 percent, and its 2026 edition 20. These are reported figures: they come from Gallup's publications and are not recomputed here.

Two habits follow. Name the edition every time. "Gallup says 23 percent" on a slide made in 2024 was current; the same slide reused in 2026 is two editions stale, and the figure has moved three points since. Do not hold it beside your own score as a comparison. Gallup's figure is the share of people its own items and its own classification call "engaged"; your survey's favourable share on your own items is a different measurement, and the two can be quoted on the same page only as two different things.

Self-studyTwo sentences that travel without a measured source3 min▶

"People leave managers, not companies." It is usually quoted as a Gallup finding and usually attached to a survey result about managers. It traces to a book, Buckingham and Coffman, First, Break All the Rules (1999), not to a measured Gallup statistic. It is not stated as fact on any page here. Apex's constructed exit reasons illustrate why it is hard to measure at all: 23 of the 210 leavers carry the category Management/Culture, but that is whatever the person filling the leaving form chose from a dropdown, and a category is a choice, as Foundations session 2 showed with "regretted".

"Replacing someone costs 1.5 to 2 times their salary." It usually arrives beside an attrition figure to make it expensive. SHRM's own range is 50 to 200 percent of salary, which is a spread of four to one, and the 1.5 to 2 times version is attributed to an analyst rather than measured. It is not stated as fact on any page here either.

Neither sentence is necessarily false. Each is unmeasured, and a survey result presented beside an unmeasured sentence borrows the sentence's confidence. The test is one question: what was counted, by whom, and in which year? Gallup's 23 has an answer; the two sentences do not.

Real world

The benchmark slide that aged. A board pack opens with "globally, only 23 percent of employees are engaged (Gallup)" and a company score beside it. The number was right for Gallup's 2024 edition. Two years later the same slide is still in the pack, the 2026 edition says 20, and nobody can say which edition the original came from because the slide never named one. Gallup's editions are real and reported; the board pack is a constructed example of how they drift.

Build-along

The survey worksheet, on paper

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code tonight. Six worksheet steps that take a survey result and turn it into a sentence you could defend: the population and its date, the response rate, the distribution of one item, the two shares, the minimum group applied to every cut, and the sentence. The worked example is the constructed survey on constructed Apex, with team B's item from Part 2; deep-dive session 7 rebuilds the survey from a seed and computes its reliability. Work each step for a survey of your own as we go.

Step 1 · Write the population and its date. Who was the survey sent to, counted on which date, by which definition of the stock. Contractors in or out; people on leave in or out. Apex: everyone on the 1 January 2026 payroll, 1,022, constructed.

★ Step 1 · the invited populationinvited: 1,022 (Apex payroll, 1 January 2026, constructed) in the population: ________________________________________ not in it: ________________________________________ yours: ______ counted on: ______________

Step 2 · Write who answered, and compare them to who did not. The response count, the rate, and one comparison of respondents against the invited on something HR already knows about everyone, such as department or tenure.

★ Step 2 · the response rate, and who is missinganswered: 600 of 1,022 = 58.7 percent (a chosen count, constructed) did not answer: 422 compare on: department / entity / tenure band (circle one) the group that answered least: ______________ its rate: ____ percent

Step 3 · Write the distribution of one item, not its mean. The percent of answers at each of the five points. Constructed team B: 20, 5, 5, 15 and 55 percent from 1 to 5.

★ Step 3 · the shapeanswer: 1 2 3 4 5 team B: 20% 5% 5% 15% 55% (constructed) yours: ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ item: ______________

Step 4 · Write the two shares, then the mean. Favourable is 4 or 5; unfavourable is 1 or 2. The mean goes last, and only beside the shares. Team B: 70 and 25, mean 3.8, the same mean as team A's 80 and 0.

★ Step 4 · favourable, unfavourable, meanfavourable (4 or 5): 70 percent (team A: 80) unfavourable (1 or 2): 25 percent (team A: 0) mean: 3.8 (team A: 3.8, the same) yours: ____ / ____ / ____

Step 5 · Apply the minimum group, then check the subtraction. Write the threshold. Hide every group below it. Then check that no hidden group can be recovered from a shown total minus the shown groups; if one can, hide one more.

★ Step 5 · the wallminimum group: 8 (a stated choice) HR by entity: 21 12 7 total shown: 40 below the threshold: 7 hide it recoverable? 40 - 21 - 12 = 7 yes, so also hide the 12 hidden remainder: 19 >= 8, safe

Step 6 · The sentence. The two shares, the item, the response count and rate, and the date. If a benchmark sits beside it, name the edition; if a folk claim is about to follow it, leave the claim out. No sentence says why.

★ Step 6 · the sentence"On [item], 70 percent answered favourably and 25 percent unfavourably, of 600 people who answered out of 1,022 invited on 1 January 2026 (58.7 percent). Groups under 8 are not shown. For context, Gallup's 2026 edition reported 20 percent of employees engaged globally, on its own items, not ours."

Keep the order. Population, response, distribution, shares, wall, sentence. A survey result read in that order is hard to misreport; a survey result that starts from the mean and works backwards is usually defending a number rather than describing one.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Foundations session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A survey was sent to 1,022 people and 600 answered. The dashboard shows "engagement 3.8". What is the first thing to add?

A survey score is a flow over a stock like any other people rate. The response rate is its base, and a change in the rate is a change in who the score describes.

2 · Two teams both average 3.8 on the same item. Team A is 80 percent favourable and 0 unfavourable; team B is 70 favourable and 25 unfavourable. What does that tell you?

A five-point mean is an average of ordered labels, not a temperature. The same 3.8 comes from very different distributions, and the two shares carry what the mean throws away.

3 · A report hides HR in Apex Logistics (7 responses) under a minimum group of 8, but shows HR overall (40) and HR in the other two entities (21 and 12). Is the 7 protected?

The minimum group applies to every cut and every difference between cuts. Hiding a cell is only protection if nothing shown lets it be rebuilt.
